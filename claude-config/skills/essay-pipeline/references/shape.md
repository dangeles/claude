# Shape

Goal: an ordered list of sections, each with a reason to exist.

## Propose a structure

Suggest a section breakdown and say why that order. Starting points by claim type — adapt
rather than apply:

**Argument** — hook, the common narrative, the core claim, evidence, counterarguments, what
follows.

**Explainer** — why this matters, what you need to know first, the core explanation, the
caveats, where it goes next.

**Prediction** — where we are, what's changing, the projection, what would make it wrong,
what it means if it's right.

Hybrids are common. Say which sections are doing argumentative work and which are doing
explanatory work, because they read differently.

## No length targets

Do not assign word counts to sections, propose a total length, or check that section
budgets sum. A section is the right length when it has made its point. If the user asks for
a target because Substack or a deadline demands it, take the number and don't mention it
again.

What replaces the word budget is proportion, judged semantically: the strongest idea should
get the most room. If background is swallowing the argument, say that — "you're spending
most of this essay on setup and the actual claim arrives at the end" — not in words, in
emphasis.

## Push on audience

The structural questions worth asking:

- Who is reading this, and what do they already believe?
- Does the reader have what they need before each section, or does section 4 depend on
  something you explain in section 5?
- Where does a skeptical reader bail? Usually it's before the payoff.
- Is the hook doing work, or is it throat-clearing?
- Does the strongest material arrive while they're still reading?

## Done when

There's a list of sections, each with a one-line purpose, in an order that makes sense to
both of you. Put it in the draft file above the thesis. It's a working document — expect it
to change while drafting, and let it.
