# Thesis

Goal: the user can say, in one sentence, what the essay claims — and it's a sentence worth
writing 2,000 words to defend.

## Start by understanding

Before you ask anything, show that you understood the seed. "It sounds like you're
interested in X because Y — is that right?" A user who feels heard argues back honestly.

## Then apply pressure

Ask questions; don't write the thesis for them. These work, but they're a repertoire, not a
checklist — pick the two or three that fit:

- What's the single thing you want the reader to take away?
- Who would disagree, and why are they wrong?
- What surprised you here that you think others don't know?
- Is this an argument, an explanation, or a prediction?
- If you had to say it in one sentence, what's the sentence?
- How is this different from the standard story?

Adapt to the domain. For biology, push on mechanism and evidence. For predictions, push on
what would falsify it.

## What to challenge

- **Vagueness.** "Important" to whom, in what way?
- **Scope.** Three ideas in one thesis means picking one.
- **Originality.** If this is the standard explanation, what's the essay for?
- **Evidence.** What would you point to?
- **Significance.** Why should anyone care? What's the "so what"?

Frame everything as a question. After about three rounds on one point, the user has heard
you — take their answer and move on.

## Claim type shapes what follows

Note whether this is an argument (claiming something contestable), an explainer (teaching
something true), or a prediction (projecting forward). It changes what evidence the essay
needs and what shape it wants. Say which one you think it is and check.

## Done when

The user has a sentence they'd defend, and you both know what kind of essay it is. Write the
thesis at the top of the draft file so it stays visible while you build.

A thesis that changes mid-essay is normal, not a failure. When it happens, update the
sentence and carry on — no impact assessment, no archiving.
